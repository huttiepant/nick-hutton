Active Thinking

Tools for thinking for yourself: a series of experiments in using technology to make us think more, not less.

Most technology, and most AI, helps us consume more and think less. It's never been easier to get an answer without understanding it, or to finish a book without remembering it. There's early research suggesting this has a cost: an MIT study found that people who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed weaker brain engagement and often couldn't recall what they'd written, and a Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon survey found that the more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they did.

These projects try the opposite. The AI asks; you do the thinking. Then it pushes back on what you came up with.

Second Order

A coach for deeply understanding what you read

Read an article, then explain it in your own words using a structured thinking framework. The coach challenges your interpretation and reasoning, points out what you missed, and asks about second-order effects. You refine your response until it's accurate, clear and genuinely yours.

Mort

A reading companion for any book

Capture your thoughts as you read, get questioned on the book's big ideas, and work through difficult passages with an assistant that knows your books. Over time you build a record of what you've learned, questioned and changed your mind about.

Private prototype

Agora

Read great books with a guide, and with other people

Read classic books annotated by a philosophy professor, starting with Marcus Aurelius's Meditations. Answer questions at the end of each chapter and discuss the ideas with other readers, so you understand the book rather than just finish it.

The principle: effort, then ownership

What these projects share comes from the learning science on how understanding is built: putting ideas into your own words, recalling things rather than rereading them, and doing work that feels hard in the moment. Research calls these desirable difficulties. A tool that removes the effort also removes the learning.

The second part is that the work you do while reading (your notes, interpretations and questions) should belong to you. It shouldn't disappear into a feed or get trapped in an AI chat. Over time I'd like these tools to use that record to test what you remember, prompt you to apply ideas rather than collect them, and show you how your thinking has changed.

The open question

I built these for myself, and they feel like they help. But feeling like you're learning isn't the same as learning, and brain-training apps are a warning about how wide that gap can be.

So the honest position is: I don't know yet whether this kind of practice builds lasting ability, or how you'd measure it. That's what I'd most like to find out. If it turns out to improve my own thinking a little, that's worth it on its own.
